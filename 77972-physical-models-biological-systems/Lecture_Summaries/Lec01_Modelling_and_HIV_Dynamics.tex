\chapter[Hidden HIV turnover]{Hidden HIV turnover}\label{chap:lecture01}
\noindent\textbf{Lecture 1 \enspace|\enspace 13 October 2026}

A constant viral load can conceal rapid production and removal. Blocking new infections exposes those processes: a two-variable model explains the initial shoulder and later decline, while showing what viral-load data cannot identify.

\section{Steady amounts and hidden turnover}\label{sec:why-model}
The modelling principles in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} lead to a concrete question: can a constant measured amount tell us how rapidly its contents are replaced?

\begin{definition}{Steady state and turnover}{steady-turnover}
A steady state has constant macroscopic variables despite possible ongoing fluxes. For an amount $W$, input $Q_{\mathrm{in}}$, and output $Q_{\mathrm{out}}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:container-balance}
\dot W=Q_{\mathrm{in}}-Q_{\mathrm{out}},\qquad
\dot W=0\Rightarrow Q_{\mathrm{in}}=Q_{\mathrm{out}}.
\end{equation}
The common flux can be large or small. Turnover is replacement of the contents while their amount can remain constant.
\end{definition}
A biological steady state can consume energy and exchange matter; it need not be thermodynamic equilibrium.

\paragraph{Physical picture: the leaky container.}
For liquid height $h$, cross-section $A$, density $\rho$, mass $m=\rho Ah$, and gravitational acceleration $g$, force balance gives
\begin{align}
p_{\mathrm{bottom}}A&=p_{\mathrm{top}}A+mg,\label{eq:hydrostatic-balance}\\
\Delta p&=\rho gh.\label{eq:hydrostatic-pressure}
\end{align}
For a viscous Poiseuille outlet, $Q_{\mathrm{out}}\propto\Delta p\propto h\propto W$. This assumes fixed cross-section and an appropriate flow regime; an arbitrary hole need not obey this law.

\Needspace{13\baselineskip}
With constant input and $Q_{\mathrm{out}}=\kappa W$ ($\kappa>0$, units time$^{-1}$),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:container-dynamics}
\dot W=Q_{\mathrm{in}}-\kappa W,\qquad W^*=Q_{\mathrm{in}}/\kappa.
\end{equation}
Subtract the steady equation and integrate $\dd(W-W^*)/(W-W^*)=-\kappa\dd t$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:container-perturbation}
\boxed{W(t)=W^*+[W(0)-W^*]\ee^{-\kappa t}.}
\end{equation}
Deviations decay on time scale $1/\kappa$: higher volume increases removal and restores the level. This solution is exact for the linear outlet law. For example, $Q_{\mathrm{in}}=2\,\mathrm{mL/min}$ and $\kappa=0.1\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ give $W^*=20\,\mathrm{mL}$ and relaxation time $10\,\mathrm{min}$ (added illustration).

\section{The experiment behind the equations}\label{sec:hiv-perturbation}
\textbf{Question.} Untreated HIV can show an acute peak, a long approximately constant viral load, and a later rise. Does the middle stage mean slow replication? Alan Perelson's modelling and David Ho's treatment experiments challenge that interpretation: measured RNA load fell roughly a hundredfold over a week to ten days after protease inhibition. A perturbation reveals turnover that a steady measurement hides.

\paragraph{Minimum biological background.}
Transcription copies DNA into messenger RNA; ribosomes translate its sequence into an amino-acid chain, which folds into a protein. HIV is a \emph{retrovirus}: its RNA is reverse-transcribed into DNA, which can integrate into the host genome. Infected cells use that information to make viral components. Copying errors create variants; selection changes their relative abundances.

\emph{Ritonavir} inhibits HIV protease, which cuts viral polyproteins into functional components. Already infected cells can still release RNA-containing particles, but impaired maturation prevents those new particles from infecting further cells in the ideal model. Mutations altering the protease's structure can reduce drug binding and confer resistance.

\textbf{Combination therapy: the probability argument.} If $n$ distinct resistance mutations each have probability $p\ll1$ per replication opportunity and are independent and required together, their joint probability is $p^n$. For $M$ opportunities, the expected number is $Mp^n$. With $M=10^9$, $p=10^{-5}$, $Mp=10^4$ but $Mp^3=10^{-6}$ (added toy example). This simultaneous-mutation model omits sequential evolution, selection, and cross-resistance; it illustrates independent barriers rather than determining a regimen.

\section{Build and solve the model}\label{sec:hiv-model}
\subsection{Variables and assumptions}\label{subsec:hiv-variables}
Time $t=0$ marks treatment. These variables denote expected populations; large populations can approximate them with small relative fluctuations.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.20\linewidth}p{0.74\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
Symbol & Meaning and units\\
\midrule
$N_I,N_{I0}$ & Infected-cell count and initial value (cells).\\
$N_V,N_{V0}$ & Free-particle count and initial value (virions).\\
$k_I,k_V$ & Per-capita cell and particle clearance (time$^{-1}$).\\
$\gamma$ & Production (virions cell$^{-1}$ time$^{-1}$).\\
$\beta=\gamma N_{I0}$ & Initial production (virions time$^{-1}$).\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Here $N_V$ includes released particles that cannot infect cells after treatment. Infectious and noninfectious particles are not distinguished. RNA copies per millilitre measure abundance, not infectivity: comparison requires a fixed conversion to the model observable. Concentrations may replace counts if all source terms are per unit volume.

\textbf{Assume:} steady viral load just before treatment; instantaneous, complete blocking of new infections; constant production per infected cell and clearance hazards; a well-mixed measured compartment. Reservoirs and cell heterogeneity are omitted. Pretreatment balance gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hiv-presteady}
\boxed{\beta=\gamma N_{I0}=k_VN_{V0}.}
\end{equation}
This constrains the initial condition; production subsequently decreases.

\subsection{From biological rules to differential equations}\label{subsec:infected-cells}
A cell's removal probability during $\Delta t$ is $k_I\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$. Conditional on the current population, expected loss is $k_IN_I\Delta t$ to leading order. Taking expectations and $\Delta t\to0$ gives the first equation below. Individual realizations fluctuate; their mean obeys the linear ODE exactly.
\begin{align}
\dot N_I&=-k_IN_I,\label{eq:infected-ode}\\
\int_{N_{I0}}^{N_I(t)}\frac{\dd n}{n}&=-k_I\int_0^t\dd s,
\qquad \ln\frac{N_I(t)}{N_{I0}}=-k_It.\label{eq:infected-integral}
\end{align}
Exponentiating gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infected-solution}
\boxed{N_I(t)=N_{I0}\ee^{-k_It}.}
\end{equation}
Constant per-cell hazard gives exponential survival of the source. Production is $\gamma N_I=\beta\ee^{-k_It}$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:viral-ode}
\boxed{\dot N_V+k_VN_V=\beta\ee^{-k_It},\qquad N_V(0)=N_{V0}.}
\end{equation}
Production declines as infected cells disappear; it does not switch off immediately.

\Needspace{25\baselineskip}
\subsection{Integrating factor: the derivation to reproduce}\label{subsec:integrating-factor}
For $\dot y+a(t)y=f(t)$: multiply by $\mu=\ee^{\int a(t)\dd t}$, recognize $(\mu y)'$, and integrate from the initial time. Here $\mu=\ee^{k_Vt}$:
\begin{align}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(\ee^{k_Vt}N_V)&=\beta\ee^{(k_V-k_I)t},\label{eq:product-rule}\\
\ee^{k_Vt}N_V(t)-N_{V0}
&=\beta\int_0^t\ee^{(k_V-k_I)s}\dd s\notag\\
&=\frac{\beta}{k_V-k_I}[\ee^{(k_V-k_I)t}-1].\label{eq:integrating-factor-integral}
\end{align}
Multiply by $\ee^{-k_Vt}$; for $k_V\ne k_I$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:viral-general-solution}
\boxed{N_V=N_{V0}\ee^{-k_Vt}
+\frac{\beta}{k_V-k_I}(\ee^{-k_It}-\ee^{-k_Vt}).}
\end{equation}
\Needspace{9\baselineskip}
The two modes combine particle clearance and loss of the source. Insert $\beta=k_VN_{V0}$:
\begin{proposition}[unbreakable]{Viral load from an initially steady state}{viral-steady}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:viral-steady-solution}
\frac{N_V}{N_{V0}}=\frac{k_V\ee^{-k_It}-k_I\ee^{-k_Vt}}{k_V-k_I}.
\end{equation}
Two clearance time scales shape one observed trajectory.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{Initial shoulder.}
Evaluate the ODE and its derivative using pretreatment balance:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:initial-derivatives}
\dot N_V(0)=\beta-k_VN_{V0}=0,\qquad
\ddot N_V(0)=-k_I\beta=-k_Ik_VN_{V0}<0.
\end{equation}
The curve starts horizontally and immediately bends down; it is not flat over a finite interval. Its early expansion is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:early-time}
N_V/N_{V0}=1-\tfrac12k_Ik_Vt^2+O(t^3).
\end{equation}
Curvature and the Taylor expansion supplement the lecture's shoulder argument.

\paragraph{Equal rates: remove the apparent singularity.}
Set $k_I=k_V=k$ before integration: $(\ee^{kt}N_V)'=\beta$, hence $\ee^{kt}N_V-N_{V0}=\beta t$, giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:equal-rate-general}
\boxed{N_V=(N_{V0}+\beta t)\ee^{-kt}.}
\end{equation}
A polynomial prefactor replaces the distinct modes. For $\beta=kN_{V0}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:equal-rate-steady}
N_V=N_{V0}(1+kt)\ee^{-kt},\qquad
\dot N_V=-N_{V0}k^2t\ee^{-kt}.
\end{equation}
\begin{remark}
% Corrects the transcript's claim at 01:56:24--01:57:00.
Equal rates do not remove the shoulder: the initial slope is still zero, consistent with official notes Eq.~1.9. A shoulder does not establish unequal rates.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Late decline and time scales.}
If $k_V\gg k_I$, particles quickly track the source: $N_V\simeq(\beta/k_V)\ee^{-k_It}$ after the fast transient. If $k_I\gg k_V$, the source disappears first and the tail decays with rate $k_V$. Thus the late logarithmic slope tends to $-\min(k_I,k_V)$, including the equal-rate case asymptotically. For a single exponential,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decay-scales}
\tau=1/k,\qquad t_{1/2}=\ln2/k,\qquad
r_{\mathrm{app}}=\frac{\ln[N(t_1)/N(t_2)]}{t_2-t_1}.
\end{equation}
$\tau$ is mean lifetime under constant hazard; an interval's apparent rate $r_{\mathrm{app}}$ need not equal either mechanistic rate. A hundredfold fall in ten days gives $r_{\mathrm{app}}=0.461\,\mathrm{day}^{-1}$ and apparent half-life $1.51$ days (added calculation).

\begin{example}[unbreakable,fontupper=\small]{Worked HIV calculation: two rates, one curve}{hiv-worked}
Added problem: initially steady, $k_I=1\,\mathrm{day}^{-1}$, $k_V=2\,\mathrm{day}^{-1}$. Thus $\beta/N_{V0}=2\,\mathrm{day}^{-1}$. In Eq.~\eqref{eq:viral-steady-solution}, set $T=1\,\mathrm{day}$:
\begin{align}
N_V/N_{V0}&=2\ee^{-t/T}-\ee^{-2t/T},\label{eq:worked-curve}\\
\dot N_V(0)/N_{V0}&=-2/T+2/T=0,\label{eq:worked-slope}\\
N_V(T)/N_{V0}&=2/\ee-1/\ee^2\simeq0.600.\label{eq:worked-value}
\end{align}
Late: $N_V/N_{V0}\simeq2\ee^{-t/T}$. The tail measures the slower rate; cancellation of slopes creates the shoulder. Exchanging rate labels preserves the curve.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Checks and common pitfalls.}
ODE terms must have units population/time; the solution must return $N_{V0}$ at $t=0$; balance fixes the initial slope. A negative mode coefficient is allowed: the total is nonnegative, as the equivalent integral shows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:viral-positive-integral}
N_V=N_{V0}\ee^{-k_Vt}+\int_0^t\beta\ee^{-k_Is}\ee^{-k_V(t-s)}\dd s\geq0.
\end{equation}

\section{What can an exam answer conclude?}\label{sec:fit-identifiability}
\begin{definition}{Structural identifiability}{identifiability}
A parameter is structurally identifiable if ideal observations distinguish it from other choices. This differs from whether noisy, finite data constrain it accurately.
\end{definition}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Count parameters:} measured $N_{V0}$ leaves $\beta,k_I,k_V$; pretreatment balance leaves two rates.
% The symmetry analysis supplements the transcript's fitting discussion.
\item \textbf{Check ambiguity:} Eq.~\eqref{eq:viral-steady-solution} is invariant under $k_I\leftrightarrow k_V$. Viral load alone can at best identify the unordered pair. Cell measurements or justified biological information must assign labels. Also $\beta=\gamma N_{I0}$ does not separate its two factors.
\item \textbf{Validate:} count independent features, not raw points; nearby measurements may be correlated. More independent constraints than parameters permit a test, but do not guarantee identifiability. Overfitting means fitting noise with excess flexibility.
\item \textbf{Respect scope:} rapid decline supports rapid hidden turnover. This model omits long-term disease progression and latent reservoirs. Dormant infected cells can retain viral DNA without producing particles, so undetectable blood RNA does not imply eradication. Reservoir activation was discussed as exploratory, not established by this calculation.
\end{itemize}
