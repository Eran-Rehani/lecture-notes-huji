\chapter[Cell kinetics and information]{Cell kinetics and information}\label{chap:lecture02}
\noindent\textbf{Lecture 2 \enspace|\enspace 20 October 2026}

This lecture answers one question in two languages. The first half asks how a population can sit at a steady value while its contents are replaced at a furious rate, and shows how a labelled pulse turns that hidden flux into a measurable travelling wave. The second half builds the vocabulary needed to say how much one measurement tells us about another: probability, entropy, mutual information, and the Kullback--Leibler divergence.

A tracer measurement reduces uncertainty about otherwise hidden dynamics. The link is conceptual; the compartment fit below does not itself calculate Shannon information.

\section{A steady state can hide a torrent}\label{sec:lec02:steady}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection*{Biological Motivation}
Blood looks static. A blood count taken today and ten years ago gives nearly the same percentages of lymphocytes, neutrophils, and monocytes, and clinicians exploit that stability: a deviation from a person's baseline is a signal. Yet the same blood is being rebuilt continuously. Of order $10^{11}$ cells are produced and die every day, all of them descending from a small pool of stem cells in the bone marrow.

The cell types are organised as a branching tree. Haematopoietic stem cells divide and differentiate into a common myeloid progenitor and a common lymphoid progenitor. The myeloid branch supplies neutrophils, monocytes, erythrocytes, and platelets; the lymphoid branch supplies B cells, T cells, and natural killer cells. Surface proteins are what make the branches visible to an experiment: CD4 marks helper T cells (the cells HIV infects), CD8 marks cytotoxic T cells that kill infected cells on contact, and natural killer cells kill cells that fail to display their identity molecules at all. These markers are functional, not decorative, which is why a pattern of surface proteins is a meaningful coordinate for a cell's state.

The biological question is quantitative: \emph{how fast} does a cell move from one state to the next? The rates matter clinically. Leukaemias are characterised by overproduction or accumulation of particular cell types, so a measured set of healthy transition rates supplies the baseline against which a diseased marrow can be compared.

Modelling the biology directly is hopeless. Inside the marrow there is spatial structure, cell--cell contact, migration between marrow, spleen, and lymph nodes, and a biochemistry that is only partly known. The lecture makes a deliberate substitution: \emph{do not model the biology; model the flow of a dye through the space of cell states}. The dye is a DNA label, and the space is the space of surface-marker profiles. The resulting model is physical (a conservation statement for a tagged quantity) rather than biochemical.

\section{Pulse-chase: turning a steady state into a signal}\label{sec:lec02:pulse-chase}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection*{Conceptual Background}
A steady state has $\dd W/\dd t=0$, so a measurement of $W$ alone reveals nothing about the fluxes feeding and draining it (Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture01}). To see dynamics one must perturb the system and watch the perturbation move. This is the logic of a pulse-chase experiment.

\begin{definition}{Pulse-chase experiment}{pulse-chase}
In a \emph{pulse}, a cohort of cells is tagged at a known time, usually while they are synthesising DNA. In the \emph{chase}, the tag is followed through later times and through cell states. The tag is not the biological process; it is a conserved marker whose redistribution reports the process.
\end{definition}

The tag used here is \emph{5-iodo-2$'$-deoxyuridine} (IdU), a thymidine analogue. A cell synthesising DNA can incorporate IdU during the pulse, so the tracer marks the DNA-synthesising cohort. Division shares labelled DNA between the daughters: on average, label per daughter is half the mother's. A cell that stops dividing and merely differentiates retains its label under the model's conservation assumption. This distinction determines what the model may assume, and we return to it in Section~\ref{sec:lec02:data}.

The readout is single-cell mass cytometry. Cells are nebulised into an argon plasma at several thousand kelvin, where they are vaporised, atomised, and ionised. The ion cloud passes through a time-of-flight mass spectrometer, which separates ions by mass-to-charge ratio. Because the plasma destroys the cell, every measurement is a snapshot of one cell. Antibodies conjugated to metal isotopes label surface proteins, and the iodine atom of IdU is counted directly. One run therefore returns, for each of millions of cells, a vector of roughly thirty marker intensities, a coordinate in ``cell space'' plus the label content at that coordinate.

\subsection{Why a pulse reveals a flux}\label{subsec:lec02:why-pulse}
In the unperturbed marrow, the number of cells in any state is constant because a large influx from upstream balances differentiation out of the state and death. The label changes nothing about those numbers; it only marks a subset. Immediately after the pulse, the labelled cells sit in the states that were dividing at that moment. As time passes, the labelled cohort moves downstream, and the label appears in states that were initially unlabelled. The total cell number in each state stays constant; only the labelled fraction moves.

A static count cannot measure a rate. The label acts as a tracer in a flow, exactly as a dye injected into a pipe reveals the flow speed without changing the pipe's contents.

\subsection{What the model may assume}\label{subsec:lec02:assumptions}
The model rests on four assumptions, which should be stated before writing its equations.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Steady state.} Cell numbers in each bin are approximately constant during the chase; incoming cells replace those leaving.
\item \textbf{Nonperturbing tracer.} At the chosen dose, IdU is assumed not to appreciably change survival, division, or differentiation. This is an experimental assumption to check with controls, not a property guaranteed by the label's name.
\item \textbf{Label conservation per cell.} A cell that neither divides nor dies carries a fixed amount of label. In the post-mitotic part of the tree this holds, because differentiation changes a cell's identity, not its DNA content.
\item \textbf{Well-mixed bins.} Cells with similar marker profiles are treated as one compartment, so a single transfer rate summarises their behaviour.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}
The label-conservation assumption is the reason the analysis is restricted to cells below the dividing compartment. In the stem and transit-amplifying region, division dilutes the label and would masquerade as loss. Section~\ref{sec:lec02:data} explains that ambiguity.
\end{remark}

\section{The source--destination model}\label{sec:lec02:model}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection*{Mathematical Setup}
Pick two neighbouring regions of cell space: an upstream \emph{source} with label content $S(t)$ and a downstream \emph{destination} with label content $D(t)$. Both are measured as mean label per cell in the bin, so they carry the same arbitrary iodine-signal units. The readout is mass cytometry, not fluorescence. Label enters the destination from the source at a rate proportional to how much labelled source is available, and leaves at a per-capita rate $d$.

The assumption of a constant per-capita exit rate gives the balance law
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:pulse-model}
\boxed{\frac{\dd D}{\dd t}=kS(t)-dD(t),\qquad D(t_0)=D_0.}
\end{equation}
Here $k$ is an effective source coefficient and $d$ an effective destination-loss coefficient, both with units of inverse time. The term $kS$ has units of label per cell per time, as required.
Read the equation from left to right: $\dot D$ is the net change in the destination's mean label, $kS$ is the incoming contribution driven by the measured source, and $dD$ is the outgoing contribution proportional to the label already present. A large $S$ raises the influx; a large $D$ raises the loss. The initial condition $D(t_0)=D_0$ states where the response begins and is required in addition to the differential equation. A zero derivative means $kS=dD$ at that instant; it does not imply that label has stopped moving.

\paragraph{Which quantity is conserved?}
Mean label per cell and total label in a bin are different observables. For constant bin populations $n_S,n_D$, cell influx $F$ from source to destination, and destination outflux $F_{\mathrm{out}}$, conservation of tracer gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:normalisation}
\frac{\dd(n_DD)}{\dd t}=FS-F_{\mathrm{out}}D,
\qquad k=\frac{F}{n_D},\quad d=\frac{F_{\mathrm{out}}}{n_D}.
\end{equation}
For clarity, $n_DD$ is total destination label, whereas $D$ is label divided by destination cell count. A cell influx $F$ carries mean label $S$ per cell, so it delivers $FS$ label units per time; outflux $F_{\mathrm{out}}$ similarly removes $F_{\mathrm{out}}D$. With $n_D$ constant, the product rule reduces to $\dd(n_DD)/\dd t=n_D\dot D$. Dividing the balance by $n_D$ gives
\begin{align}
n_D\dot D&=FS-F_{\mathrm{out}}D,\notag\\
\dot D&=\frac{F}{n_D}S-\frac{F_{\mathrm{out}}}{n_D}D=kS-dD.\label{eq:lec02:per-cell-balance}
\end{align}
If $n_D$ changed in time, an extra term $D\dot n_D$ would remain and this simple reduction would fail. If the intrinsic source-exit rate is $a=F/n_S$, then $F=an_S$ and $k=a n_S/n_D$. Thus a fitted $k$ is not automatically a source cell's differentiation rate. This bookkeeping example assumes no division or other sources; extra pathways or inconsistent signal normalisation change the interpretation.

\begin{definition}{Transfer function of a compartment}{transfer-function}
For a linear, time-invariant compartment, the zero-initial-state input--output rule can be represented by an \emph{impulse-response kernel}. Here it is $K(\tau)=k\ee^{-d\tau}$ for $\tau\geq0$, zero for $\tau<0$, acting as a convolution. Its Laplace transform is the \emph{transfer function}. The classroom's time-domain description uses the same linear-filter idea.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Solution by integrating factor}\label{subsec:lec02:solution}
Equation~\eqref{eq:lec02:pulse-model} is first order because its highest derivative is $\dot D$, and linear in $D$ because it contains neither $D^2$ nor products of $D$ with its derivatives. The measured $S(t)$ is treated as a known input. Move the loss term to the left, $\dot D+dD=kS(t)$. Following Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture01}, choose a multiplier whose derivative is $d$ times itself: $\mu(t)=\ee^{dt}$. The product rule gives $\dd(\ee^{dt}D)/\dd t=\ee^{dt}\dot D+d\ee^{dt}D$, exactly the required pair of terms:
\begin{align}
\ee^{dt}\dot D+d\ee^{dt}D&=k\ee^{dt}S(t),\label{eq:lec02:product-rule}\\
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\bigl(\ee^{dt}D\bigr)&=k\ee^{dt}S(t).\label{eq:lec02:exact-derivative}
\end{align}
Integrate from $t_0$ to $t$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:integrated}
\ee^{dt}D(t)-\ee^{dt_0}D_0=k\int_{t_0}^{t}\ee^{dt'}S(t')\,\dd t'.
\end{equation}
Multiplying by $\ee^{-dt}$ gives two simplifications: the initial term becomes $\ee^{-dt}\ee^{dt_0}D_0=\ee^{-d(t-t_0)}D_0$, and the integrand acquires $\ee^{-dt}\ee^{dt'}=\ee^{-d(t-t')}$. Thus

\begin{proposition}[unbreakable]{Source--destination solution}{source-destination}
For constant $k$ and $d$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:pulse-solution}
D(t)=\underbrace{k\int_{t_0}^{t}\ee^{-d(t-t')}S(t')\,\dd t'}_{\text{label arriving from the source}}
+\underbrace{\ee^{-d(t-t_0)}D_0}_{\text{label already present}}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

The integration variable $t'$ denotes the time a contribution entered; $t$ is the later observation time. The elapsed residence time is $t-t'\geq0$. In a short interval $\dd t'$, the source supplies $kS(t')\dd t'$ label units per cell, of which the fraction $\ee^{-d(t-t')}$ remains at $t$. Integration adds the surviving contributions from every entry time. The separate $D_0$ term accounts for label present before those new entries. At $t=t_0$, the integral is zero and the remaining term is exactly $D_0$. If $k=0$, only the initial signal decays; if $d=0$, all incoming label accumulates without loss.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:transfer-kernel}
\boxed{D=K*S\quad\text{if }D_0=0,\qquad K(\tau)=k\,\ee^{-d\tau}\ \ (\tau\geq0).}
\end{equation}
The destination is a causal, exponentially forgetting filter of the source. Recent source label dominates; label injected a time $\gg1/d$ ago has already leaked out.

\subsection{Why the integral form is the useful one}\label{subsec:lec02:why-integral}
Mathematically, Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:pulse-solution} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:pulse-model} are equivalent: differentiate the former and the latter returns. Numerically differentiating noisy observations and fitting an integrated prediction handle measurement error differently, however.

Suppose the measured label has independent noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ at each time point. A finite-difference estimate of the derivative uses
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:finite-difference}
\dot D(t_i)\approx\frac{D_{i+1}-D_i}{\Delta t},
\qquad \text{error}\ \sim\frac{\sigma\sqrt2}{\Delta t}.
\end{equation}
Specifically, write each measurement as $D_i=D_{\mathrm{true}}(t_i)+\eta_i$, where $\eta_i$ has zero mean and variance $\sigma^2$. The finite-difference noise is $(\eta_{i+1}-\eta_i)/\Delta t$. Independence makes its variance $(\sigma^2+\sigma^2)/(\Delta t)^2$; taking the square root gives the stated standard deviation. Correlated errors would contribute an additional covariance term and change this expression.
Shrinking $\Delta t$ to improve resolution amplifies the noise without bound. In the language of Fourier analysis, differentiation multiplies each frequency component by $\ii\omega$: it is a high-pass filter and it amplifies precisely the noise. Integration multiplies by $1/(\ii\omega)$: it is a low-pass filter, and it averages the noise over the history. That is the lecture's point in one line: \emph{a derivative of noisy data is noise; an integral of noisy data is an average}.

There is a second, more important reason. The fit never needs $\dot D$ at all. Given the measured source $S(t)$ and trial values of $(k,d)$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:pulse-solution} predicts the entire destination curve. Comparing prediction with measurements avoids explicitly differentiating the noisy destination series. Independent cohorts remove repeated measurements of the same animal, but do not guarantee uncorrelated errors: batch effects, calibration, and uncertainty in the source curve can remain. A $1/\sqrt{N}$ improvement applies to particular averages of independent errors, not universally to this fitted model. Fit uncertainty must reflect the sampling and measurement process.

\begin{remark}
The correct-but-useless equation is a recurring theme in biological modelling. Equation~\eqref{eq:lec02:pulse-model} is exact under its assumptions, but estimating its derivative from a few dozen mice is hopeless. ``In biology it is not enough to be right; you have to be smart and specific,'' as the lecturer put it. The transfer-function rewrite is the smart version.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Supplementary: the transfer function in the frequency domain}\label{subsec:lec02:frequency}
For this subsection choose the time origin $t_0=0$. Define the Laplace transform $\tilde D(s)=\int_0^\infty\ee^{-st}D(t)\dd t$, where $s$ has units of inverse time. Integration by parts explains the transformed derivative, for $s$ such that the boundary term at infinity vanishes:
\begin{align}
\int_0^\infty\ee^{-st}\dot D(t)\dd t
&=\bigl[\ee^{-st}D(t)\bigr]_0^\infty+s\int_0^\infty\ee^{-st}D(t)\dd t\notag\\
&=-D(0)+s\tilde D(s).\label{eq:lec02:laplace-derivative}
\end{align}
With zero initial label, transforming the balance law and collecting the terms in $\tilde D$ gives $(s+d)\tilde D=k\tilde S$. Dividing by $s+d$ yields
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:laplace}
s\tilde D(s)=k\tilde S(s)-d\tilde D(s)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\tilde D(s)=\frac{k}{s+d}\,\tilde S(s).
\end{equation}
The ratio $\tilde D/\tilde S=k/(s+d)$ is dimensionless because $k$, $d$ and $s$ all have units of inverse time. For a sinusoidal input, set $s=\ii\omega$, where $\omega$ is angular frequency. Since $|d+\ii\omega|=\sqrt{d^2+\omega^2}$, taking the complex modulus gives the gain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:gain}
\left|\frac{\tilde D}{\tilde S}\right|=\frac{k}{\sqrt{d^2+\omega^2}}.
\end{equation}
For $d>0$, source fluctuations with $\omega\ll d$ have approximately constant gain $k/d$; faster ones are attenuated as $1/\omega$. The compartment therefore smooths the source history on a time scale $1/d$. A sharply pulsed source produces a broad destination wave, and the delay of the peak carries information about $d$ rather than about $k$.

\subsection{Supplementary: fitting the rates}\label{subsec:lec02:fitting}
For this fitting example assume $D_0=0$, negligible error in the interpolated source, and independent destination errors with equal variance. For fixed $d$, the predicted destination is linear in $k$: $D_{\mathrm{pred}}(t_i)=k\,G_d(t_i)$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:G}
G_d(t_i)=\int_{t_0}^{t_i}\ee^{-d(t_i-t')}S(t')\,\dd t'.
\end{equation}
Here $D_i$ is the measured destination signal at time $t_i$ and $G_i=G_d(t_i)$ is the integrated source prediction for a trial $d$. Their difference $D_i-kG_i$ is a residual. The unweighted sum of squared residuals, denoted $\chi^2$ here, measures mismatch; a dimensionless statistical chi-square would additionally divide each squared residual by its measurement-error variance. Expand the square and differentiate with respect to $k$, holding $d$ and therefore all $G_i$ fixed:
\begin{align}
\chi^2(k)&=\sum_iD_i^2-2k\sum_iD_iG_i+k^2\sum_iG_i^2,\notag\\
\frac{\dd\chi^2}{\dd k}&=-2\sum_iD_iG_i+2k\sum_iG_i^2=0,\notag\\
k\sum_iG_i^2&=\sum_iD_iG_i,\qquad
\frac{\dd^2\chi^2}{\dd k^2}=2\sum_iG_i^2>0.\label{eq:lec02:least-squares-steps}
\end{align}
The positive second derivative confirms a minimum when the source gives a nonzero design. Equivalently, adding and subtracting $(\sum_iD_iG_i)^2/\sum_iG_i^2$ completes the square:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:chi2}
\chi^2=\Bigl(\sum_iG_i^2\Bigr)\Bigl(k-\frac{\sum_iD_iG_i}{\sum_iG_i^2}\Bigr)^2
+\sum_iD_i^2-\frac{\bigl(\sum_iD_iG_i\bigr)^2}{\sum_iG_i^2}.
\end{equation}
With the physical constraint $k\geq0$, the minimising transfer rate and residual are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:fit-k}
\boxed{k_*(d)=\frac{\max(0,\sum_iD_iG_d(t_i))}{\sum_iG_d(t_i)^2},
\qquad
\chi^2_{\min}(d)=\sum_iD_i^2-\frac{\max(0,\sum_iD_iG_d(t_i))^2}{\sum_iG_d(t_i)^2}.}
\end{equation}
These formulas require $\sum_iG_i^2>0$: a source with no observed signal cannot determine $k$. If noisy or baseline-corrected data give a negative unconstrained optimum, the constrained minimum is at $k=0$ with residual $\sum_iD_i^2$. For known nonzero $D_0$, first subtract $\ee^{-d(t_i-t_0)}D_0$ from each $D_i$ for every trial $d$; otherwise fit the initial condition too. Unequal error variances require weighted least squares. Only $d$ remains nonlinear in the zero-initial-state example: scan it, or minimise numerically over nonnegative rates. This ``profile'' strategy (solve the linear parameter exactly, search over the nonlinear one) is the standard way to fit small compartment models.

\subsubsection*{Method}
To extract rates from a pulse-chase data set:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose a source bin and a destination bin in marker space, and extract $S_i$ and $D_i$ at times $t_i$.
\item Fix a trial $d$ and compute $G_d(t_i)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:G}, e.g.\ by the recursion of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:discrete}.
\item Compute $k_*(d)$ and $\chi^2_{\min}(d)$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:fit-k}.
\item Minimise $\chi^2_{\min}(d)$ over $d$; report $(k_*,d)$ and the residual.
\item Check units ($k,d$ in $\text{time}^{-1}$), positivity, and that the residual has no systematic trend in time.
\end{enumerate}

For numerical work on a uniform grid, let $t_{n+1}-t_n=\Delta t$, $D_n=D(t_n)$, and approximate the source by the constant $S_n$ on this interval. Apply the integrated solution with initial time $t_n$. For $d>0$,
\begin{align}
D_{n+1}&=\ee^{-d\Delta t}D_n+kS_n\int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}}\ee^{-d(t_{n+1}-t')}\dd t',\notag\\
\int_{t_n}^{t_{n+1}}\ee^{-d(t_{n+1}-t')}\dd t'
&=\int_0^{\Delta t}\ee^{-du}\dd u
=\frac{1-\ee^{-d\Delta t}}{d}.\label{eq:lec02:discrete-integral}
\end{align}
In the second line, $u=t_{n+1}-t'$ reverses the limits and $\dd t'=-\dd u$ reverses them back. The result is the exact interval update
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:discrete}
D_{n+1}=\ee^{-d\Delta t}D_n+\frac{kS_n}{d}\bigl(1-\ee^{-d\Delta t}\bigr).
\end{equation}
This recursion is exact for the stated piecewise-constant input. At $d=0$, use its continuous limit $D_{n+1}=D_n+kS_n\Delta t$. Refining the time grid checks the approximation to a varying measured source.

\begin{example}[unbreakable,fontupper=\small]{Worked example: peak delay and amplitude}{fit-rates}
Added problem. A pulse produces a source that decays exponentially, $S(t)=S_0\ee^{-t/T}$ with $T=10\,\mathrm{h}$, and the destination has $d=0.2\,\mathrm{h}^{-1}$, $k=0.3\,\mathrm{h}^{-1}$. Find $D(t)$, the time of the peak, and the peak height.

With $D(0)=0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:pulse-solution} gives
\begin{align}
D(t)&=kS_0\int_0^t\ee^{-d(t-t')}\ee^{-t'/T}\dd t'
 =kS_0\ee^{-dt}\int_0^t\ee^{(d-1/T)t'}\dd t',\notag\\
&=kS_0\ee^{-dt}\frac{\ee^{(d-1/T)t}-1}{d-1/T}\notag\\
&=\frac{kS_0}{d-1/T}\bigl(\ee^{-t/T}-\ee^{-dt}\bigr),\label{eq:lec02:worked-D}\\
&=\frac{0.3S_0}{0.1}\bigl(\ee^{-t/10}-\ee^{-0.2t}\bigr)
=3S_0\bigl(\ee^{-t/10}-\ee^{-0.2t}\bigr).\label{eq:lec02:worked-numbers}
\end{align}
Differentiate each exponential and set the result to zero. Cancelling the nonzero prefactor $kS_0/(d-1/T)$ and rearranging gives
\begin{align}
0&=-\frac1T\ee^{-t_*/T}+d\ee^{-dt_*},\notag\\
dT&=\ee^{(d-1/T)t_*},\qquad
\ln(dT)=(d-1/T)t_*,\notag\\
t_*&=\frac{\ln(dT)}{d-1/T}=\frac{\ln2}{0.1\,\mathrm{h}^{-1}}=6.93\,\mathrm{h}.\label{eq:lec02:peak-time}
\end{align}
The logarithm's argument $dT$ is dimensionless. The signal initially rises from zero, and decays at late times, so this unique stationary point is its maximum.
At that time $\ee^{-t_*/T}=0.5$ and $\ee^{-dt_*}=0.25$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:peak-height}
D_{\max}=3S_0(0.5-0.25)=0.75\,S_0.
\end{equation}
The delay $t_*$ depends on $d$ and on the source decay $T$, not on $k$; the transfer rate $k$ sets the amplitude. For this fixed source shape, changing $k$ changes only the amplitude; the peak time constrains $d$. In a multistage pathway, a later source pulse can also cause a later destination peak.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
Check the answer in two limits. If $d=1/T$, use L'H\^opital: $D\to kS_0t\,\ee^{-t/T}$, which peaks at $t_*=T$. If $d\gg1/T$, the destination empties almost instantly and $t_*\to 1/d\ln(dT)\to0$: the peak follows the pulse immediately. Both limits are physically sensible.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Experimental Design}
Mice receive an intraperitoneal IdU pulse, followed by bone-marrow sampling at different chase times. The published experiment used $48$ mice, with $2$--$3$ animals per time point across $1$--$228$\,h. These source-verified details refine the classroom's schematic cohort description. Matching animal backgrounds and accounting for collection batches matter because biological and technical differences can alter the measured trajectories.

The measured marker vector has about thirty dimensions. In the neutrophil example discussed in class, two coordinates are the surface-marker signals CD11b and Ly6G, displayed on logarithmic axes. Colour in a cell-density map indicates how many cells occupy a marker region, not elapsed time or IdU content. A one-dimensional developmental path through this space permits binning; a principal-component direction summarises variance and need not be the developmental trajectory itself. Cells are binned along that path, and each bin gets its own time series. The path is a proxy for developmental progression, not a literal physical coordinate. A time trace then follows the mean label in one fixed marker bin across independently sampled animals, not the motion of one tracked cell.

The specific rates are a by-product.

\subsection{Consistency checks and caveats}\label{subsec:lec02:caveats}
\paragraph{Label budget.} Because the same label passes through successive bins, the time-integrated signals of neighbouring bins should be comparable. Integrating the balance equation with $D(0)=D(\infty)=0$, $d>0$, and convergent integrals gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:area}
\int_0^\infty D(t)\,\dd t=\frac{k}{d}\int_0^\infty S(t)\,\dd t.
\end{equation}
To see every step, integrate $\dot D=kS-dD$ over the whole chase. The integral of a derivative is an endpoint difference:
\begin{align}
D(\infty)-D(0)&=k\int_0^\infty S(t)\dd t-d\int_0^\infty D(t)\dd t=0,\notag\\
d\int_0^\infty D(t)\dd t&=k\int_0^\infty S(t)\dd t.\label{eq:lec02:area-balance-steps}
\end{align}
Division by $d$ gives the area relation. The integral of a signal over time has units signal times time; it is not an instantaneous cell count or a cell flux.
Equal areas therefore require $k/d\simeq1$, not equal $d$ across bins. A closed, constant-population destination with its only influx from the chosen source has $F=F_{\mathrm{out}}$ and hence $k=d$ under the preceding normalisation. Additional pathways, death, finite observation windows, or changed normalisation can spoil the comparison. The lecture's approximate area check is useful only with these qualifications.

\paragraph{Division dilutes the label.} On average, division shares labelled DNA between two daughters: mean label per daughter is half the mother's, while total label across the daughters is conserved if no label is lost. Declining per-cell signal can therefore reflect division rather than departure or death. A universal extra exponential-loss rate cannot be inferred without specifying division timing and how cells are sampled. Restricting analysis to post-mitotic cells avoids this ambiguity.

\paragraph{A continuum, not discrete types.} The density of cells in marker space is continuous: immunologists draw boundaries between ``cell types'' where the density has valleys, but the underlying developmental path is a continuum. Binning is a discretisation of that continuum, and the bin width trades resolution against the number of cells per bin. With tens of thousands of cells per subtype, a few dozen bins per path is a reasonable compromise.

\paragraph{Population, not single cell.} A single cell would produce a sharp pulse at one time. The measured peak is broad because the cohort is asynchronous: some cells were slightly ahead, some behind. The width of the peak is therefore a measure of biological heterogeneity, not of measurement noise alone. This also answers a natural objection (``how can the same cell be in two bins?''): the experiment never tracks one cell; it tracks the same \emph{stage} in different animals.

\section{Beyond two variables: infectious and non-infectious virions}\label{sec:lec02:hiv4}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection*{Motivation}
The two-variable HIV model of Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture01} treated treatment as an abrupt block of new infections and lumped all free particles into one variable. That was enough to explain the shoulder and the decline. It is not enough to say what a protease inhibitor actually does, because such a drug does not stop infected cells from releasing particles; it prevents those particles from being infectious. A slightly larger model makes the distinction explicit and, in doing so, introduces the first genuinely nonlinear interaction in the course.

\subsection{The model}\label{subsec:lec02:hiv4-model}
Let $N_U$ be uninfected target cells (CD4 T cells), $N_I$ infected cells, $N_V$ infectious virions, and $N_X$ non-infectious virions. Uninfected cells are produced at rate $\lambda$ and die at rate $k_U$; infected cells die at $k_I$; both classes of virion are cleared at $k_V$. Infection requires a collision between a virion and a target cell, so its rate is proportional to the product $N_VN_U$.

\end{figure}

With these ingredients the model is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:hiv-eqs}
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\dot N_U&=\lambda-k_UN_U-\epsilon N_VN_U,\\
\dot N_I&=\epsilon N_VN_U-k_IN_I,\\
\dot N_V&=\epsilon'\gamma N_I-k_VN_V,\\
\dot N_X&=(1-\epsilon')\gamma N_I-k_VN_X.
\end{aligned}}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:lec02-params} collects the parameters and their units.

\paragraph{Construct each term from a biological event.}
Define the infection flux $J(t)=\epsilon N_VN_U$, in cells newly infected per time. In a well-mixed compartment, one target cell has infection hazard $\epsilon N_V$; multiplying by the number of targets gives $J$. Over a sufficiently short interval, its infection probability is $\epsilon N_V\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$. This is the mass-action assumption: encounter opportunities increase with both populations.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Uninfected cells:} $\lambda$ is a supply independent of the current target count; $k_UN_U$ is ordinary target-cell death; $J$ removes cells from the uninfected class by infecting them. Hence $\dot N_U=\lambda-k_UN_U-J$.
\item \textbf{Infected cells:} the same $J$ enters this class with a plus sign, while $k_IN_I$ removes infected cells by death or clearance. Thus $\dot N_I=J-k_IN_I$. For example, $N_I(t+\Delta t)-N_I(t)=[J(t)-k_IN_I(t)]\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$; division by $\Delta t$ and the limit give the differential equation.
\item \textbf{Infectious particles:} all infected cells together produce $\gamma N_I$ particles per time, but only the fraction $\epsilon'$ is infectious. Production is therefore $\epsilon'\gamma N_I$; clearance is $k_VN_V$. These contribute opposite signs in $\dot N_V$.
\item \textbf{Noninfectious particles:} the remaining fraction $1-\epsilon'$ contributes $(1-\epsilon')\gamma N_I$, and clearance removes $k_VN_X$. This model assumes the same particle-clearance rate for both classes.
\end{enumerate}
Infection changes a cell's class rather than instantly adding or destroying a cell. Adding the first two equations cancels $J$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:total-cell-balance}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(N_U+N_I)=\lambda-k_UN_U-k_IN_I.
\end{equation}
This sign check would fail if infection were accidentally given the same sign in both equations. The particle equations omit explicit virion consumption during an infection event; they approximate particle loss by the clearance terms shown. Production creates particles without transferring a cell into the particle population.

First, $\epsilon$ is \emph{not} a probability or a fraction: it multiplies $N_VN_U$, so it must carry whatever units make the product a rate. If populations are measured as concentrations, $\epsilon$ has units of volume per virion per time. Second, the system is linear in each variable separately but bilinear in the pair $(N_V,N_U)$; that single product is enough to destroy the closed-form solutions of Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture01}.

\begin{remark}
% Flags a genuine slip in the transcript.
The spoken explanation briefly interchanges the infection coefficient and the infectious fraction. Dimensional analysis resolves the ambiguity: $\epsilon$ is the mass-action coefficient with units, and $\epsilon'$ is the dimensionless infectious fraction.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Supplementary: fixed points and the infection threshold}\label{subsec:lec02:fixed}
This added derivation assumes positive production and clearance parameters, $\epsilon>0$, and $0<\epsilon'\leq1$; the treated limit $\epsilon'=0$ is considered separately below. Setting all derivatives to zero gives two kinds of steady state. The first is the uninfected state,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:uninfected}
N_V=N_I=N_X=0,\qquad N_U=\lambda/k_U,
\end{equation}
To derive this branch, set $N_V=0$ in $0=\epsilon N_VN_U-k_IN_I$, which forces $N_I=0$. Then $0=(1-\epsilon')\gamma N_I-k_VN_X$ forces $N_X=0$, while $0=\lambda-k_UN_U$ gives $N_U=\lambda/k_U$. Target cells sit at their production--loss balance and no virus persists. A fixed point means that populations are constant; individual supply and loss terms can still be nonzero.

The second branch is the chronic infected state. From $\dot N_V=0$ and $\dot N_I=0$, move each loss term to the other side and divide by its positive coefficient:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:fixed-I}
N_I=\frac{k_VN_V}{\epsilon'\gamma},\qquad
N_I=\frac{\epsilon N_VN_U}{k_I},
\end{equation}
Equate the two expressions for $N_I$ to eliminate it. At a positive infected fixed point $N_V\ne0$, cancellation of $N_V$ is allowed:
\begin{align}
\frac{k_VN_V}{\epsilon'\gamma}&=\frac{\epsilon N_VN_U}{k_I},\notag\\
k_Ik_VN_V&=\epsilon\epsilon'\gamma N_VN_U,\notag\\
k_Ik_V&=\epsilon\epsilon'\gamma N_U.\label{eq:lec02:fixed-U-steps}
\end{align}
Divide by $\epsilon\epsilon'\gamma$ to obtain
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:fixed-U}
\boxed{N_U^*=\frac{k_Ik_V}{\epsilon\epsilon'\gamma}.}
\end{equation}
At the infected steady state the target-cell number is set entirely by the viral parameters; it does not depend on the supply rate $\lambda$. Supply and infection balance only at one particular target density.

Find the remaining populations in order. The target-cell balance gives
\begin{align}
0&=\lambda-k_UN_U^*-\epsilon N_V^*N_U^*,\notag\\
\epsilon N_V^*N_U^*&=\lambda-k_UN_U^*,\notag\\
N_V^*&=\frac{\lambda-k_UN_U^*}{\epsilon N_U^*}
=\frac1\epsilon\left(\frac\lambda{N_U^*}-k_U\right).\label{eq:lec02:fixed-V-steps}
\end{align}
Substitute this $N_V^*$ into $N_I^*=k_VN_V^*/(\epsilon'\gamma)$. Finally, $0=(1-\epsilon')\gamma N_I^*-k_VN_X^*$ implies $N_X^*=(1-\epsilon')\gamma N_I^*/k_V$; inserting $N_I^*$ cancels $\gamma$ and $k_V$. Collecting the results,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:fixed-rest}
N_V^*=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\Bigl(\frac{\lambda}{N_U^*}-k_U\Bigr),
\qquad
N_I^*=\frac{k_VN_V^*}{\epsilon'\gamma},
\qquad
N_X^*=\frac{1-\epsilon'}{\epsilon'}N_V^*.
\end{equation}
An algebraic fixed point is biologically admissible only if its populations are nonnegative. Since $\epsilon>0$, positivity of $N_V^*$ requires a positive bracket. Insert the derived $N_U^*$ and rearrange only positive factors:
\begin{align}
\frac{\lambda}{N_U^*}&>k_U,\notag\\
\frac{\lambda\epsilon\epsilon'\gamma}{k_Ik_V}&>k_U,\notag\\
\frac{\lambda\epsilon\epsilon'\gamma}{k_Uk_Ik_V}&>1.\label{eq:lec02:threshold-steps}
\end{align}
The dimensionless ratio on the left is called $\mathcal R_0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:R0}
\boxed{\mathcal R_0\equiv\frac{\lambda\,\epsilon\,\epsilon'\gamma}{k_U\,k_I\,k_V}>1.}
\end{equation}
$\mathcal R_0$ is the expected number of new infected cells produced by one infected cell when targets are at their uninfected level $N_U^{(0)}=\lambda/k_U$. The positive chronic state exists only above threshold.

\paragraph{Why the same threshold controls initial growth.}
Let $i$ and $v$ be small infected-cell and infectious-particle populations near the uninfected state. In the infection product, replacing $N_U$ by $N_U^{(0)}$ is correct to first order: the product of $v$ with a small target deviation is second order. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:infection-linearisation}
\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix}i\\v\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-k_I&\epsilon N_U^{(0)}\\\epsilon'\gamma&-k_V\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}i\\v\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
To find a growth or decay mode, set $(i,v)=(i_0,v_0)\ee^{rt}$. The growth rate $r$ has units of inverse time. A nonzero mode requires the determinant of the coefficient matrix minus $r$ times the identity to vanish:
\begin{align}
0&=(r+k_I)(r+k_V)-\epsilon\epsilon'\gamma N_U^{(0)}\notag\\
&=r^2+(k_I+k_V)r+k_Ik_V(1-\mathcal R_0).\label{eq:lec02:growth-characteristic}
\end{align}
The two roots have sum $-(k_I+k_V)<0$. Their product is $k_Ik_V(1-\mathcal R_0)$: below threshold both have negative real parts, while above threshold a negative product gives one positive root and hence growth. At $\mathcal R_0=1$ one root is zero, so this linear argument alone does not establish decay. This is a local invasion test, not a prediction of the complete disease course.

The derivation of $\mathcal R_0$ from the parameters is a useful check on intuition: one infected cell lives a time $1/k_I$ and produces $\epsilon'\gamma/k_I$ infectious virions; each virion lives $1/k_V$ and infects $\epsilon N_U$ target cells per unit time, so it infects $\epsilon N_U/k_V$ cells. The product of these two factors is $\epsilon\epsilon'\gamma N_U/(k_Ik_V)$, which equals Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:R0} once $N_U=\lambda/k_U$ is inserted.

\begin{example}[unbreakable,fontupper=\small]{Worked example: does the chronic state exist?}{hiv-fixed-point}
Added problem. Take $\lambda=10^4$ cells\,mL$^{-1}$\,day$^{-1}$, $k_U=0.1$\,day$^{-1}$, $k_I=1$\,day$^{-1}$, $k_V=20$\,day$^{-1}$, $\gamma=10^3$ virions\,cell$^{-1}$\,day$^{-1}$, $\epsilon=10^{-7}$\,mL\,virion$^{-1}$\,day$^{-1}$, and $\epsilon'=0.1$.

Uninfected target density: $N_U^{(0)}=\lambda/k_U=10^5$\,mL$^{-1}$. Threshold:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:worked-R0}
\mathcal R_0=\frac{10^4\times10^{-7}\times0.1\times10^3}
{0.1\times1\times20}=\frac{0.1}{2}=0.05<1.
\end{equation}
The chronic state does not exist; the virus is cleared. To see what would change it, use $\mathcal R_0\propto\lambda\epsilon\epsilon'\gamma$: a tenfold increase gives only $0.5$, still below threshold. A $25$-fold larger supply, or a $25$-fold longer infected-cell lifetime, gives $1.25>1$. Increasing the infectious fraction alone cannot cross threshold in this example, since $\epsilon'\leq1$ permits at most a tenfold increase. A drug that lowers $\epsilon'$ therefore acts twice: it reduces $\mathcal R_0$, and it reduces infectious virion production directly.
\end{example}

\subsection{What treatment does in this language}\label{subsec:lec02:treatment}
A protease inhibitor impairs maturation, so newly budded particles are non-infectious. In the model this is $\epsilon'\to0$: the branch to $N_V$ closes and all production goes to $N_X$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:treated}
\dot N_V=-k_VN_V,\qquad N_V(t)=N_V(0)\ee^{-k_Vt},
\end{equation}
The infectious solution follows by the same separation $\dd N_V/N_V=-k_V\dd t$ used in Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture01}. Meanwhile infected cells continue to die at $k_I$ and noninfectious particles continue to be made and cleared. This is the decay of \emph{infectious} particles, not the total RNA/particle load. To compare with Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture01}, define $L=N_V+N_X$. Add the two particle equations before making the treatment approximation:
\begin{align}
\dot L&=\epsilon'\gamma N_I-k_VN_V+(1-\epsilon')\gamma N_I-k_VN_X\notag\\
&=[\epsilon'+(1-\epsilon')]\gamma N_I-k_V(N_V+N_X)\notag\\
&=\gamma N_I-k_VL.\label{eq:lec02:load-sum-steps}
\end{align}
The fractions add to one: changing infectious quality alone does not instantly remove particle production. Together with the infected-cell equation,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:total-load}
\dot L=\gamma N_I-k_VL,
\qquad \dot N_I=\epsilon N_VN_U-k_IN_I.
\end{equation}
Pre-existing infectious particles can still infect cells while they clear. If that residual infection is negligible, then $\dot N_I\simeq-k_IN_I$ and the two-variable model follows for $(N_I,L)$. Its load has both time scales and can retain the initial shoulder. In Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture01}, the symbol $N_V$ denoted this lumped particle population; here $N_V$ denotes only the infectious part.

These fixed-parameter equations do not explain the eventual progression to AIDS. Chronic balance and the existence threshold are model results; a later collapse of immune function requires additional biological mechanisms. A change in the fixed point must not be asserted without specifying which model parameters or processes change.

\begin{remark}
Nonlinear systems of this type generally have no closed-form solution. Existence, uniqueness, and stability are local statements; numerical integration is the standard tool, and the course returns to nonlinear dynamics later. The linear techniques of Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture01} are a special case, not the general rule.
\end{remark}

\section{Probability, quickly but carefully}\label{sec:lec02:probability}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection*{Motivation}
The second half of the lecture builds the language of randomness. The class is heterogeneous (not everyone arrives from physics), so the material is a fast review, but every definition is used later, and the exam assumes fluency.

\begin{definition}{Bernoulli trial}{bernoulli}
A \emph{Bernoulli trial} has two outcomes, coded $s=1$ (success) and $s=0$ (failure), with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:bernoulli}
\Pr(s=1)=\xi,\qquad \Pr(s=0)=1-\xi,\qquad 0\leq\xi\leq1.
\end{equation}
For a fair coin $\xi=1/2$. The two probabilities sum to one because the outcomes are exhaustive and mutually exclusive.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Binary fractions and the uniform distribution}\label{subsec:lec02:binary}
Toss a fair coin twice and form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:binary-fraction}
x=\frac{s_1}{2}+\frac{s_2}{4}.
\end{equation}
The four outcomes $(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)$ give $x=0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34$, each with probability $\tfrac14$. With $m$ tosses,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:binary-general}
x_m=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{s_i}{2^i}
\end{equation}
takes $2^m$ values, each with probability $2^{-m}$: a uniform distribution on the dyadic fractions in $[0,1)$. As $m\to\infty$ the histogram of $x_m$ approaches a continuous uniform density. The base $2$ is a convention, not a law: decimal digits $d_i\in\{0,\dots,9\}$ with weights $10^{-i}$ generate a uniform distribution in the same way.

Figure~\ref{fig:lec02-binary-histograms} shows the distinction between a theoretical probability and a finite-sample estimate. Independent fair bits give any particular sequence probability $\prod_{i=1}^m(1/2)=2^{-m}$. For equally spaced histogram bins of width $\Delta x=2^{-m}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:binary-histogram-normalisation}
P(x_m=j/2^m)=2^{-m},\qquad
\frac{P(x_m=j/2^m)}{\Delta x}=1,\qquad j=0,\ldots,2^m-1.
\end{equation}
The displayed bar heights are probability masses: adding more bins reduces the mass per bin. Rescaling by bin width gives the unit-height uniform density; it does not change total probability.

\subsection{Probability mass functions and events}\label{subsec:lec02:pmf}
\begin{definition}{Probability mass function}{pmf}
For a discrete random variable $X$ taking values $\ell$, the \emph{probability mass function} is $P(\ell)=\Pr(X=\ell)$, normalised by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:pmf}
P(\ell)\geq0,\qquad \sum_{\ell}P(\ell)=1.
\end{equation}
Probabilities are dimensionless numbers; the normalisation is the statement that something must happen.
\end{definition}

For events $E_1,E_2$,
\begin{align}
P(E_1\cup E_2)&=P(E_1)+P(E_2), && E_1\cap E_2=\varnothing,\label{eq:lec02:union}\\
P(E_1\cup E_2)&=P(E_1)+P(E_2)-P(E_1\cap E_2), &&\text{in general},\label{eq:lec02:inclusion}\\
P(E^{c})&=1-P(E).\label{eq:lec02:complement}
\end{align}
The subtraction in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:inclusion} removes the double-counted overlap.

\begin{definition}{Conditional probability and independence}{conditional}
For $P(E')>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:conditional}
P(E\mid E')=\frac{P(E\cap E')}{P(E')}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently $P(E\cap E')=P(E\mid E')P(E')$. The events are \emph{independent} when conditioning does not change the probability:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:independent}
P(E\mid E')=P(E)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P(E\cap E')=P(E)P(E').
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\paragraph{A die, conditioned.} A fair die gives $P(5)=1/6$. If we learn that the result is odd, the sample space shrinks to $\{1,3,5\}$ and $P(5\mid\text{odd})=(1/6)/(1/2)=1/3$. Knowledge changed the relevant ensemble; it did not change the die.

\paragraph{Bayes' theorem.} The product rule read in both directions gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:bayes}
P(H\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H)P(H)}{P(E)},
\qquad
P(E)=\sum_jP(E\mid H_j)P(H_j)
\end{equation}
for mutually exclusive, exhaustive hypotheses $H_j$. The denominator is the law of total probability. Bayes' theorem is not a separate principle; it is the product rule plus normalisation.

\subsection{Waiting for success: the geometric distribution}\label{subsec:lec02:geometric}
\begin{definition}{Geometric distribution}{geometric}
Repeat independent Bernoulli trials with success probability $0<\xi\leq1$ until the first success. If $J$ is the index of that success, then $J=j$ requires $j-1$ failures followed by one success:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:geometric}
P(J=j)=\xi(1-\xi)^{j-1},\qquad j=1,2,\dots
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Normalisation is a geometric series:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:geometric-norm}
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\xi(1-\xi)^{j-1}
=\xi\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(1-\xi)^{k}
=\frac{\xi}{1-(1-\xi)}=1.
\end{equation}
The mean follows by differentiating the series with respect to $q=1-\xi$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:geometric-mean}
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}j\,\xi q^{j-1}
=\xi\frac{\dd}{\dd q}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}q^{j}
=\xi\frac{\dd}{\dd q}\frac{q}{1-q}
=\frac{\xi}{(1-q)^2}
=\frac{1}{\xi}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[unbreakable]{Mean waiting time}{geometric-mean}
For a geometric waiting time, $\mathbb E[J]=1/\xi$. A rare event with success probability $\xi\ll1$ requires of order $1/\xi$ attempts.
\end{proposition}
This bridges a per-attempt probability and a time scale. If infection is attempted at rate $r$ with success probability $\xi$ per attempt, the mean wait is $1/(\xi r)$; the inverse of a rate is a time, as it must be.

\begin{remark}
With $\xi=0.1$, the mean is $10$ trials, and the probability of waiting more than $10$ trials is $(0.9)^{10}=0.35$. The distribution has a long tail (roughly a third of cases exceed the mean) because the geometric distribution is memoryless and heavily skewed. Its variance is $(1-\xi)/\xi^2$ (added).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Joint, marginal, and conditional distributions}\label{subsec:lec02:joint}
For two discrete variables,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:joint}
P_{XY}(x,y)=\Pr(X=x,\,Y=y),\qquad
P_X(x)=\sum_yP_{XY}(x,y),\qquad
P_Y(y)=\sum_xP_{XY}(x,y).
\end{equation}
The sums are the \emph{marginals}: they discard one variable. Independence is precisely the factorisation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:factorisation}
P_{XY}(x,y)=P_X(x)P_Y(y).
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[unbreakable,fontupper=\small]{Worked example: marginals and a conditional}{marginal-table}
Added problem with an illustrative joint distribution. Let $X\in\{0,1\}$ record whether a cell experienced a nutrient pulse and $Y\in\{0,1\}$ whether its reporter is high, with
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
 & $Y=0$ & $Y=1$ & $P_X$\\
\midrule
$X=0$ & $0.40$ & $0.20$ & $0.60$\\
$X=1$ & $0.05$ & $0.35$ & $0.40$\\
$P_Y$ & $0.45$ & $0.55$ & $1$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Marginals come from summing rows and columns. The conditional is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:table-conditional}
P(Y=1\mid X=1)=\frac{P(X=1,Y=1)}{P(X=1)}=\frac{0.35}{0.40}=0.875.
\end{equation}
Independence fails, because $P(X=1,Y=1)=0.35\neq(0.40)(0.55)=0.22$. Knowing that the nutrient pulse occurred raises the reporter probability from $0.55$ to $0.875$.
\end{example}

\section{Entropy: quantifying surprise}\label{sec:lec02:entropy}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection*{Conceptual Background}
Probability tells us what to expect. Entropy is the expected surprisal $-\log_2 P_X(X)$, averaged over possible outcomes. The idea is Shannon's. Its resemblance to thermodynamic entropy is structural: the Gibbs entropy has the same $-\sum p\ln p$ form multiplied by Boltzmann's constant. That statistical expression is also used away from thermal equilibrium.

\begin{definition}{Shannon entropy}{entropy}
For a discrete random variable $X$ with mass function $P_X$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:entropy}
H(X)=-\sum_xP_X(x)\log_2P_X(x),
\end{equation}
with $0\log_20\equiv0$. The base $2$ makes $H$ measured in \emph{bits}; natural logarithms give nats, and the two differ by the constant factor $\ln2$.
\end{definition}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[width=0.60\linewidth,height=5.2cm,xmin=0,xmax=1,ymin=0,ymax=1.36,
  axis lines=left,xlabel={$\xi$},ylabel={$H(\xi)$ [bits]},
  tick label style={font=\footnotesize},label style={font=\small},
  xtick={0,0.5,1},ytick={0,0.5,1},samples=200,domain=0.001:0.999]
\addplot[primary,thick] {-x*ln(x)/ln(2)-(1-x)*ln(1-x)/ln(2)};
\addplot[accent,dashed,domain=0:1] {1};
\node[anchor=south,font=\scriptsize,text=primary] at (axis cs:0.5,1.04)
  {max at $\xi=\tfrac12$};
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{Entropy of a binary variable. A fair coin carries one full bit of uncertainty; a strongly biased coin carries almost none, because its outcome is nearly predictable. The curve is symmetric about $\xi=\tfrac12$ and vanishes at both endpoints.}\label{fig:lec02-entropy-binary}
\end{figure}

Three properties follow from the definition.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deterministic:} if one outcome has probability $1$, then $H=0$. There is nothing to learn.
\item \textbf{Uniform:} for $M$ equally likely outcomes, $H=\log_2M$. A fair coin gives one bit; a fair die gives $\log_26\approx2.58$ bits.
\item \textbf{Bounded:} $H\leq\log_2M$ for any distribution on $M$ outcomes, with equality only for the uniform distribution. This is the content of the maximum-entropy principle: the least informative assumption is the most uniform distribution consistent with what is known.
\end{itemize}

For a binary variable, one bit of entropy means \emph{two equally likely possibilities}, as for a fair coin, and is the maximum possible entropy. A coin that always lands heads has zero entropy: you already knew. Entropy is therefore a measure of ignorance, not of disorder in any visual sense.

\begin{remark}
Entropy is dimensionless, like probability. In Boltzmann's formula $S=k_B\ln\Omega$ the constant $k_B$ is what gives entropy its thermodynamic units; the information-theoretic quantity has no such constant.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Biological Interpretation}
Entropy of a distribution over cell states measures how uncertain we are about the state of a randomly chosen cell. If a tissue is dominated by one cell type, the entropy is low; if many types are equally abundant, it is high. This is the logic behind biodiversity indices: a community dominated by one species is less diverse than an evenly populated one with the same species list. The exponential $2^{H}$ converts entropy back into an \emph{effective number} of equally likely states, a count that is often easier to interpret than a logarithm. A culture with $H=0.47$ bits behaves, for this purpose, like one with $2^{0.47}\approx1.38$ equally likely states.

The maximum-entropy principle is used as a modelling rule: among all distributions consistent with the measurements, choose the one with the largest entropy, i.e.\ the one that assumes the least. It is a disciplined way to avoid smuggling in structure that the data do not support.

\section{Conditional entropy and mutual information}\label{sec:lec02:mi}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection*{Motivation}
Entropy measures uncertainty in one variable. The biological question is usually about two: does knowing the environment tell us what a gene will do? How much of a cell's behaviour is explained by a signal, and how much is noise? Conditional entropy and mutual information answer this quantitatively, without assuming linearity.

\subsection{Conditional entropy}\label{subsec:lec02:conditional-entropy}
\begin{definition}{Conditional entropy}{conditional-entropy}
The entropy of $Y$ given that $X=x$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:conditional-entropy-x}
H(Y\mid X=x)=-\sum_yP(y\mid x)\log_2P(y\mid x).
\end{equation}
Averaging over the distribution of $X$ gives the conditional entropy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:conditional-entropy}
H(Y\mid X)=\sum_xP_X(x)\,H(Y\mid X=x)
=-\sum_{x,y}P_{XY}(x,y)\log_2P(y\mid x).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
The average is essential: different values of $X$ may leave different residual uncertainty, and the overall statement must weight each case by how often it occurs.

\begin{remark}
A common transcription-level confusion: the logarithm in Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:conditional-entropy} contains the \emph{conditional} probability $P(y\mid x)$, not the joint. The prefactor $P_{XY}(x,y)$ is joint, which is what converts the sum into an expectation over the pair. Using the joint inside the log gives a different (and wrong) quantity.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The gene-regulation channel}\label{subsec:lec02:channel}
Consider a cell that senses food. Let $X=1$ mean food is present and $X=0$ mean it is absent, with $P(X=0)=P(X=1)=\tfrac12$: the environment is maximally uncertain. Let $Y=1$ mean a metabolic gene is switched on and $Y=0$ mean it is off. Ideally $Y=X$. In reality the response is noisy: the gene is on $90\%$ of the time when food is present and off $90\%$ of the time when it is absent. The conditional distributions are therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:channel}
P(Y=1\mid X=1)=0.9,\quad P(Y=0\mid X=0)=0.9,
\end{equation}
with the complementary errors $0.1$. The joint distribution is $P(0,0)=P(1,1)=0.45$ and $P(0,1)=P(1,0)=0.05$.

\paragraph{Without measuring $X$.} Each conditional has the same error rate, so the marginal is symmetric:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:channel-marginal}
P(Y=1)=0.5\times0.9+0.5\times0.1=0.5,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:channel-HY}
H(Y)=-\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12-\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12=1\ \text{bit}.
\end{equation}
The gene is on half the time; before looking at the environment, the outcome is a fair coin.

\paragraph{With $X$ known.} For either value of $X$, the residual uncertainty is that of a binary variable with error $0.1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:channel-h}
H(Y\mid X=x)=-0.1\log_20.1-0.9\log_20.9=0.469\ \text{bits}.
\end{equation}
The average is the same because the two cases are symmetric, so $H(Y\mid X)=0.469$ bits. Knowing the environment removes $1-0.469=0.531$ bits of uncertainty about the gene. This is Problem~2.2 of the assignments (binary symmetric channel) with flip probability $\varepsilon=0.1$: for uniform input the general result is $I(X;Y)=1-h_2(\varepsilon)$ bits, where $h_2(\varepsilon)=-\varepsilon\log_2\varepsilon-(1-\varepsilon)\log_2(1-\varepsilon)$.

\begin{example}[unbreakable,fontupper=\small]{Worked example: mutual information of a noisy switch}{gene-information}
Added problem, completing the lecture's calculation. For the channel of Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:channel},
\begin{align}
I(X;Y)&=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)=1-0.469=0.531\ \text{bits},\label{eq:lec02:worked-mi}\\
2^{H(Y\mid X)}&=2^{0.469}=1.38,\qquad
2^{I(X;Y)}=2^{0.531}=1.45.\label{eq:lec02:worked-eff}
\end{align}
Conditioning reduces the effective number of states from $2$ to about $1.38$. This is an entropy-based effective count, not a count of possible outcomes. Relative to a noiseless binary channel, the information falls from $1$ to $0.531$ bits: a loss of about $47\%$. With the stated symmetric noise and uniform input, $0.531$ bits also equals the channel capacity. For ten independent input--output pairs, conditional entropies add, giving the effective count $2^{10(0.469)}\approx26$; all $2^{10}=1024$ response strings remain possible. The effective-count reduction must not be interpreted as eliminating possible strings.

Mutual information is a difference of entropies, uncertainty before minus uncertainty after, and it is symmetric: measuring the gene tells you just as much about the food as measuring the food tells you about the gene.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
The lecture quoted $0.43$ bits at one point and $0.47$ bits later; the exact value for a $10\%$ error rate is $0.469$ bits. The value $0.43$ is a verbal or transcription slip, not a rounding of $0.469$; $0.47$ is the appropriate two-decimal rounding.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Joint entropy and the chain rule}\label{subsec:lec02:chain-rule}
To compare uncertainty before and after observing another variable, first define uncertainty about the pair:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:joint-entropy}
H(X,Y)=-\sum_{x,y}P_{XY}(x,y)\log_2 P_{XY}(x,y).
\end{equation}
Using $P_{XY}(x,y)=P_X(x)P(y\mid x)$ on the support of the joint distribution,
\begin{align}
H(X,Y)&=-\sum_{x,y}P_{XY}(x,y)\bigl[\log_2P_X(x)+\log_2P(y\mid x)\bigr]\notag\\
&=-\sum_xP_X(x)\log_2P_X(x)+H(Y\mid X)\notag\\
&=H(X)+H(Y\mid X).\label{eq:lec02:chain-rule}
\end{align}
Interchanging $X,Y$ gives $H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X\mid Y)$. Thus the pair's uncertainty consists of uncertainty about one variable plus the average uncertainty remaining about the other. Subtracting these chain rules gives the equivalent mutual-information formulas below.

\subsection{Mutual information: three equivalent forms}\label{subsec:lec02:mi-forms}
\begin{definition}{Mutual information}{mutual}
The mutual information between $X$ and $Y$ is the reduction in uncertainty about one variable obtained by measuring the other:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:mi}
I(X;Y)=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The three forms are the same statement counted three ways; Figure~\ref{fig:lec02-venn} shows the bookkeeping. The first adds both circles and subtracts the union once, which leaves the overlap. The second starts with all of $Y$ and removes the part not explained by $X$. The third does the reverse.

\subsection{The log-ratio form}\label{subsec:lec02:log-ratio}
A fourth form is useful for computation. Expand the entropy definition of $I$ as a single expectation over the joint distribution:
\begin{align}
I(X;Y)&=\sum_{x,y}P_{XY}(x,y)\bigl[\log_2P_{XY}(x,y)-\log_2P_X(x)-\log_2P_Y(y)\bigr].\label{eq:lec02:mi-expansion}
\end{align}
Combining logarithms gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:mi-sum}
\boxed{I(X;Y)=\sum_{x,y}P_{XY}(x,y)\log_2\frac{P_{XY}(x,y)}{P_X(x)P_Y(y)}.}
\end{equation}
Mutual information measures how far the joint distribution is from the product of its marginals, that is, how far the pair is from independence. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, the ratio is $1$ everywhere, every logarithm vanishes, and $I=0$. No assumption of linearity, monotonicity, or Gaussianity enters.

For the gene example, Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:mi-sum} reads
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:mi-check}
I=2(0.45)\log_2\frac{0.45}{0.25}+2(0.05)\log_2\frac{0.05}{0.25}
=0.763197-0.232193=0.531004\ \text{bits},
\end{equation}
in agreement with Eq.~\eqref{eq:lec02:worked-mi}. The agreement is a useful check that the joint table, the marginals, and the conditional entropies are mutually consistent.

\subsection{Kullback--Leibler divergence}\label{subsec:lec02:kl}
\begin{definition}{Kullback--Leibler divergence}{kl}
For two probability mass functions $P$ and $Q$ on the same outcomes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:kl}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)=\sum_xP(x)\log_2\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)},
\end{equation}
Zero-probability terms with $P(x)=0$ contribute zero. If $Q(x)=0<P(x)$, the divergence is $+\infty$; the finite case requires $Q>0$ wherever $P>0$.
\end{definition}
Supplementary interpretation: ideal code lengths $-\log_2Q(x)$ incur an expected excess of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)$ bits per observation over code lengths matched to $P$. Real finite codes need not attain these ideal lengths exactly. The divergence vanishes only when $P=Q$, and it grows as the model $Q$ becomes a worse description of the truth $P$.

Two warnings. First, it is \emph{not symmetric}: $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)\neq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\Vert P)$ in general, so it is a divergence, not a distance. Second, it does not obey the triangle inequality. Both facts follow from the logarithm: swapping the arguments changes the weighting as well as the ratio.

\begin{example}[unbreakable,fontupper=\small]{Homework 2.1: explicit Bernoulli divergence}{kl-bernoulli}
For $Q\sim\mathrm{Bern}(q)$ and $P\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p)$ with $0<p,q<1$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:kl-bernoulli}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q\Vert P)=q\log_2\frac{q}{p}+(1-q)\log_2\frac{1-q}{1-p}.
\end{equation}
Exchanging the two arguments gives $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)$, which is generally a different number: the divergence is directional. The assignment works in natural logarithms (nats); the base-2 version differs by the constant $\ln2$. Problem~2.1 also asks for the Poisson pair, whose closed form $\lambda_1\ln(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)+\lambda_2-\lambda_1$ is a useful check for later chapters.
\end{example}

\subsection{Non-negativity, and why information cannot be negative}\label{subsec:lec02:gibbs}
The proof uses Jensen's inequality.

\begin{proposition}[unbreakable]{Jensen's inequality}{jensen}
For a convex function $\phi$ and a random variable $Z$ in its domain,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:jensen}
\phi\bigl(\mathbb E[Z]\bigr)\leq\mathbb E\bigl[\phi(Z)\bigr].
\end{equation}
For concave $\phi$ the inequality reverses.
\end{proposition}

Geometrically: a convex curve lies below the chord joining two points, so the function evaluated at the mean argument is no greater than the mean of its values. Take $\phi(u)=-\log_2u$, which is convex because $\phi''(u)=1/(u^2\ln2)>0$.

\begin{proposition}[unbreakable]{Gibbs' inequality}{gibbs}
For any two probability mass functions $P,Q$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:gibbs}
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)\geq0,
\end{equation}
with equality if and only if $P=Q$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $Q(i)=0<P(i)$ for any $i$, the divergence is infinite and the result follows. Otherwise draw $i$ from $P$, restrict to its support $A=\{i:P(i)>0\}$, and set $Z=Q(i)/P(i)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:gibbs-mean}
m\equiv\mathbb E_P[Z]=\sum_{i\in A}Q(i),\qquad 0<m\leq1.
\end{equation}
Apply Jensen to $\phi(u)=-\log_2u$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:gibbs-jensen}
0\leq-\log_2m=\phi\bigl(\mathbb E_P[Z]\bigr)
\leq\mathbb E_P\bigl[\phi(Z)\bigr]
=\sum_{i\in A}P(i)\log_2\frac{P(i)}{Q(i)}
=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q).
\end{equation}
Equality requires both $m=1$ and a constant ratio $Q(i)/P(i)$ on $A$. Normalisation then makes that ratio one, so $P=Q$ on all outcomes. This support qualification fills a step left implicit in the official proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[unbreakable]{Mutual information is non-negative}{mi-nonneg}
Since
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lec02:mi-kl}
I(X;Y)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(P_{XY}\,\Vert\,P_XP_Y\bigr),
\end{equation}
mutual information satisfies $I(X;Y)\geq0$, with equality exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
\end{corollary}
Equivalently, conditioning reduces uncertainty on average: $H(Y\mid X)\leq H(Y)$. It need not reduce uncertainty for every particular value $X=x$; the expectation over $X$ is essential.

\begin{remark}
The KL divergence gives mutual information a natural form: $I$ is the divergence between the true joint distribution and the best ``independent'' model of it. KL divergence generally changes when its two distribution arguments are exchanged. Mutual information remains symmetric in the variable names: exchanging $X$ and $Y$ merely reindexes the same joint-versus-product sum.
\end{remark}

\section{What information buys, and what it costs}\label{sec:lec02:limits}
% ==============================================================================

\subsection*{Conceptual Background}
Mutual information is demanding: it detects any statistical dependence, linear or not, monotone or not. That strength is also its weakness.

\paragraph{Strengths.} It assumes no functional form. If $Y$ depends on $X$ through a threshold, a saturation, or a non-monotone response, mutual information still detects it. Correlation, by contrast, measures only linear association and can vanish for a perfectly deterministic nonlinear relationship.

\paragraph{Costs.} Computing $I(X;Y)$ requires the full joint distribution, which in practice means a two-dimensional histogram. Two marginals require far less data than one joint: the lecture's image is that estimating two one-dimensional distributions is like drawing two lines, while estimating the joint is like filling an area. In cell biology, where each measurement is expensive, the joint is often unobtainable, and a correlation coefficient (cheap, well understood, but linear) is used as a weaker substitute. In neuroscience, where electrodes record many trials quickly, the joint is feasible and information measures are standard.

\paragraph{Is information what biology optimises?} The lecture's answer is qualified. Evolution optimises survival and reproduction, not bits. Efficient transmission of information from the environment into the cell is one axis of fitness (an organism that cannot sense food or danger is quickly outcompeted), but it competes with others. The classroom contrast between organisms with different nervous systems illustrates that successful information processing need not resemble a large brain; it does not establish a ranking of evolutionary success or a forecast of future survival. Information processing is a powerful strategy, not the only one. Information is an important axis of fitness, not its definition.
